## Summary

<span>Last night’s Tennessee special election gave both Democrats and Republicans something to worry about.</span>

## Content

<p><span>In the 2024 presidential election, Donald Trump carried Tennessee’s Seventh Congressional District by 22 points. Last night, in a special election to represent the district, the Republican Matt Van Epps won by only nine points, </span><a href="https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2025/12/02/us/elections/results-tennessee-us-house-7-special.html"><span><span>defeating</span></span></a><span><span> State Representative Aftyn Behn, a Democrat. </span></span></p><p><span><span>Trump </span></span><a href="https://truthsocial.com/@realDonaldTrump/posts/115653238186658350"><span><span>celebrated</span></span></a><span><span> the outcome on Truth Social as a “BIG Congressional WIN,” but the margin of victory in a deep-red district is ominous for Republicans. Van Epps underperformed Trump by 13 percentage points, a sign that the party is vulnerable heading into the 2026 midterms. If Democrats could replicate that shift everywhere next year, they would gain upwards of 40 seats in the House and take back the Senate. </span></span></p><p><span><span>But last night’s outcome also offers Democrats a cautionary tale. An off-year special election in December is precisely the kind of low-turnout situation in which the party’s highly educated base currently dominates. In such races, Democrats probably need to run up the score by even more than 13 points before they can have a real shot at winning both houses of Congress next year. And if they’d nominated a more moderate candidate, they probably would have. </span></span></p><p><i>[<a href="https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2025/11/leftists-moderates-democrats-economy/684992/?utm_source=feed">Rogé Karma: Democrats finally realize it isn’t 2016 anymore</a>]</i></p><p><span><span>Behn, a 36-year-old former community organizer, has the kind of progressive background that might not hurt in a Democratic primary but can become a real liability in a general election—including an extensive trail of </span></span><a href="https://abcnews.go.com/Politics/deep-red-tennessee-house-district-turned-political-battleground/story?id=128001509"><span><span>quotes</span></span></a><span><span> that ended up being used against her. She </span></span><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vNuZz-gOeDc"><span><span>told</span></span></a><span><span> a Nashville interviewer in 2020, “I’m a very radical person.” In now-deleted tweets from the same year, she advocated for dissolving the Nashville police department and </span></span><a href="https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/tennessee-candidate-blasts-dem-opponent-172152100.html?guccounter=1&amp;guce_referrer=aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuZ29vZ2xlLmNvbS8&amp;guce_referrer_sig=AQAAAFUynG55mgbYan5yty8K4KKDn_0mkjSw_lv2ZZWitCfpvYJZP8t4HYXBhx6oUW87kGA2QLdbug17YJVABF-fLmr37wgk4VZX95nwyvZsFO5fsEWwfyka3Pj7WObllXUSdgX5esaMuwzxdD9E9QlqmyEcg0RoRDDY9mv1ZlHuYjsI"><span><span>wished</span></span></a><span><span> a “Good morning, especially to the 54% of Americans that believe burning down a police station is justified.” She </span></span><a href="https://fox17.com/news/local/i-hate-this-city-democratic-candidate-aftyn-behn-at-center-of-controversial-clip"><span><span>said</span></span></a><span><span> on a podcast that she hated country music, bachelorette parties, and the city of Nashville itself, and suggested on a different episode that “birthers”—a gender-neutral term for “men and women who can give birth”—should refuse to procreate as a form of “collective bargaining.” </span></span></p><p><span><span>Republican groups seized on those quotes in the final few weeks of the campaign, spending millions of dollars on attack ads to make sure as many voters in the district as possible heard them. This tactic appears to have worked to some degree. A 13-point over-performance sounds huge, but in contemporary political terms, it’s pedestrian. The president is deeply unpopular, and the Democratic coalition has grown ever whiter, older, richer, more highly educated, and more female—a recipe for high turnout in off years. Indeed, compared with other Democrats who ran in special elections for Congress this year, Behn’s performance is below average. Democrats averaged a staggering over-performance of 18 points in races that took place in Florida, Virginia, and Arizona. </span></span></p><p><span><span>The ambiguity of the Tennessee results—Behn lost, but she over-performed, but she probably under-over-performed—has reignited an interminable intra-party debate. Some members of the Democratic left argue that the key to winning elections is mobilization: nominating inspiring progressive candidates who excite the party’s voters, driving up Democratic turnout. Moderates (and plenty of more pragmatic leftists) counter that this never works, and that winning—especially in Trump districts—requires persuasion: running candidates with enough moderate positions to win over some conservative voters. </span></span></p><p><span><span>Behn’s candidacy was a test case for the first theory. “This Tennessee special congressional election is about MOBILIZATION,” the candidate </span></span><a href="https://aftynbehnfortn.substack.com/cp/175300039"><span><span>wrote</span></span></a><span><span> in October. The Democratic National Committee chair, Ken Martin, </span></span><a href="https://www.thebulwark.com/p/special-election-tennesee-7th-democrats-aftyn-behn"><span><span>told</span></span></a><span><span> </span><i><span>The Bulwark</span></i><span> last week that the race was “not about persuading voters; it’s about turning them out.” Following Behn’s loss, some progressives doubled down on that idea, </span></span><a href="https://x.com/admcrlsn/status/1996055200541671685?s=20"><span><span>arguing</span></span></a><span><span> that a moderate candidate wouldn’t have performed better than Behn did, because the base wouldn’t have turned out. They argue that if Behn didn’t do as well as other special-election candidates have, it’s because her race drew much more national attention, including from Trump himself, leading to the barrage of attack ads. Of course, those attack ads might have had less bite if the candidate hadn’t personally provided so much fodder for them. </span></span></p><p><span><span>One thing that keeps the motivation–persuasion debate going is that, in any specific electoral race, both sides have an unfalsifiable argument. Win or lose, they can argue that the Democratic candidate would have fared better if only they had tried harder to appeal to swing voters or if only they had tried harder to rock the vote.</span></span></p><p><span><span>The weight of the evidence, however, strongly favors the persuasion theory. There is no real trade-off between persuasion and turnout, because sporadic voters are not hardcore progressives waiting to be activated. In ideological terms, they are, in fact, </span></span><a href="https://decidingtowin.org"><span><span>very similar</span></span></a><span><span> to swing voters; if anything, sporadic voters are even more moderate and conservative. Because these voters are similar to each other, the same basic tactics tend to work with both groups: focusing on the economy and stressing that you have mainstream rather than far-left views about cultural issues.</span></span></p><p><i>[<a href="https://www.theatlantic.com/politics/archive/2025/09/democrats-moderation-working-class/684264/?utm_source=feed">Marc Novicoff: Democrats don’t seem willing to follow their own advice</a>]</i></p><p><span><span>Last night’s special election showed the limits of a turnout-alone strategy. If ever that approach is going to work, it is in an off-year special election. In the midterms, the Democratic turnout advantage is all but guaranteed to shrink. (This will be even truer in the 2028 presidential election.) At some point, the time will come to face the full electorate.  </span></span></p><p><span><span>In the four-way </span></span><a href="https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2025/10/07/us/elections/results-tennessee-us-house-7-special.html"><span><span>Democratic primary</span></span></a><span><span> for Tennessee’s Seventh, only 31,000 voters cast a ballot, fewer than half the number that voted for Behn yesterday. Behn won that primary with 28 percent of the vote, beating the businessman Darden Copeland by fewer than 1,000 votes. Copeland had run on lowering the national debt and wrote in a </span></span><a href="https://ballotpedia.org/Darden_Copeland#Campaign_themes"><span><span>candidate survey</span></span></a><span><span> that he models himself on Dick Gephardt, the pro-life Democratic congressman who once chaired the centrist Democratic Leadership Council. </span></span></p><p><span><span>Democrats are in a strong position heading into 2026. One of the only things standing in their way is the likelihood of them nominating more Aftyn Behns, when Darden Copelands are staring them in the face. </span></span></p>
